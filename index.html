gavel One-sided Argumentative Essay expand_more

Writing Notes

  • Purpose: To argue strictly in favor of or against a specific topic and persuade the reader to agree with that single point of view.
  • Title: Requires an attention-grabbing, brief, and specific title, which can be a question, a noun clause, a phrase, or a short sentence.
  • Structure:
    • Introduction: Outline the topic and provide background information. It should include a clear issue for discussion and the writer’s stance. A "turnabout" introduction may be used to first state the opposite view before refuting it.
    • Body: Each paragraph must cover a different idea, start with a topic sentence, and include reasons and examples for support.
    • Conclusion: Summarize the arguments and restate the opinion using different words (paraphrasing). It should end with a powerful sentence or rhetorical question to impress the reader.
rate_review Letter to the Editor expand_more

Writing Notes

  • Purpose: Usually written to respond to another letter (agreeing or disagreeing), state an opinion on a public issue, or make a complaint.
  • Tone and Style: Must be formal and objective. Avoid personal attacks and the use of "you" to refer to general readers.
  • Structure:
    • Introduction: Clearly state the issue being discussed and your position or theme.
    • Body: Include three paragraphs, each presenting one strong argument supported by reasons and starting with a clear topic sentence.
    • Conclusion: Summarize the main points, restate your position, and include a "call for action".
  • Language Focus: Use formal language (no contractions or abbreviations) and suitable adjective patterns to express ideas. Passive voice can be used to maintain objectivity.
article Feature Article expand_more

Writing Notes

  • Title: Needs to be attention-grabbing, predict the content, and be relevant. Use a phrase starting with a gerund (verb + -ing) or a question word.
  • Introduction: Offer information to let readers know what to expect. Limit to around three sentences.
  • Headings: Place before each body paragraph to separate content. Keep them short, specific, and consistent in grammatical structure.
  • Body Paragraphs: Provide relevant information and examples to support what is mentioned under each heading.
  • Conclusion: Wrap up main ideas with a call to action.

volunteer_activism Letter of Advice expand_more

Writing Notes

Three Main Parts:

  • Opening Paragraph: Show that you understand the recipient's situation and feelings.
  • Body Paragraphs: Give specific advice to address the situation. Include one main idea in each paragraph clearly shown in the topic sentences. Give reasons and examples to support your advice.
  • Closing Paragraph: Briefly summarise your suggestions and encourage the recipient to try them out.

report_problem Letter of Complaint expand_more

Writing Notes

  • Purpose: A formal letter written to express dissatisfaction and ask for a follow-up action.
  • Tone: Firm and polite.
  • Style Restrictions: You should NOT use:
    • Contractions (e.g., use "I am" instead of "I'm")
    • Abbreviations (e.g., use "Hong Kong" instead of "HK")
    • Direct speech
    • Informal punctuation (e.g., dashes, brackets)
  • Structure: Greeting → Subject → Reason for writing → Complaint(s) → Ask for action → Closing → Sender's signature → Sender's name.

campaign Promotional Speech expand_more

Writing Notes

  • Audience & Occasion: Tailor your content and tone to your audience (e.g., schoolmates) and adapt to the occasion (e.g., morning assembly).
  • Opening Statement: Grab attention, get them engaged, and let them know the purpose. Use a provocative question, relevant quote, brief personal story, or surprising fact.
  • Structure: Add topic sentences at the beginning of every point. Use repetition of keywords/themes to reinforce ideas.
  • End Memorably: Return to the strong purpose and end in a meaningful way (promoting the event, stating how they can help, or sharing a true story).

Writing Notes

  • Content: The whole blog entry centres around your experience in one inter-school competition only. Maintain a balance of content and ensure you respond to every part of the prompt.
  • Organization: Include an attention-grabbing title. Write a summary statement in the opening paragraph which draws readers' attention. End with an overall thought and use the HHH structure to show encouragement.
  • Language focus: Use simple present and past tenses correctly. Use a suitable language style and tone. Ensure pronouns are used consistently (e.g. do not mix you/they when referring to the same group). Incorporate relevant SDT (Show, Don't Tell) phrases to describe the experience.

gavel Sentence patterns used in a two-sided argumentative essay expand_more

1. Background (Introduction)

In recent years, [Topic] has become a highly debated issue in our society, with growing concerns over its impact on [Group/Aspect].

E.g. "In recent years, the rise of artificial intelligence has become a highly debated issue in our society, with growing concerns over its impact on human employment."

2. Showing Stance (Thesis Statement)

From my perspective, it is undeniably clear that [Stance] due to its overwhelming benefits / detrimental effects.

E.g. "From my perspective, it is undeniably clear that implementing a four-day workweek is necessary due to its overwhelming benefits to employee mental health."

3. Giving Examples (Body Paragraphs)

A prime example of this is [Example/Scenario], which vividly demonstrates how [Point being made].

E.g. "A prime example of this is Finland's education system, which vividly demonstrates how reducing standardized testing can actually boost academic performance."

4. Making a Summary (End of a Point/Paragraph)

To put it briefly, the evidence presented underscores the fact that [Summary of argument point].

E.g. "To put it briefly, the evidence presented underscores the fact that excessive screen time severely harms teenagers' social development."

5. Restating Stance (Conclusion)

Taking all these factors into consideration, I firmly reiterate that [Restated Stance] is the only logical course of action.

E.g. "Taking all these factors into consideration, I firmly reiterate that banning single-use plastics is the only logical course of action for a sustainable future."

newspaper Sentence patterns used in a letter to the editor expand_more

1. Background (Referencing a Trigger Event)

I am writing in response to the recent article titled "[Article Title]" published on [Date], which highlighted the rising issue of [Issue].

E.g. "I am writing in response to the recent article titled 'The Cost of Fast Fashion' published on 5th May, which highlighted the rising issue of textile waste in our city."

2. Showing Purpose of Writing

The primary purpose of this letter is to draw public attention to [Issue] and to voice my deep concerns regarding [Specific aspect].

E.g. "The primary purpose of this letter is to draw public attention to the lack of youth recreational centers and to voice my deep concerns regarding the rise in teenage tech addiction."

3. Writing a Topic Sentence (Body Paragraph)

First and foremost, it is crucial to recognize that the root cause of [Problem] lies in [Cause].

E.g. "First and foremost, it is crucial to recognize that the root cause of light pollution in Hong Kong lies in the unregulated use of commercial neon signs."

4. Making a Summary (Concluding the Arguments)

In short, the arguments raised above clearly indicate that [Core Message] requires immediate public scrutiny.

E.g. "In short, the arguments raised above clearly indicate that the proposed redevelopment of our heritage sites requires immediate public scrutiny."

5. Hope for the Future (Sign-off)

It is my sincere hope that the authorities will take swift action and that your readers will become more mindful of [Issue].

E.g. "It is my sincere hope that the authorities will take swift action and that your readers will become more mindful of their daily food waste."

menu_book Part 1: Opinions & Argumentation expand_more

(Best for: Letters to the Editor, Argumentative Essays, Editorials)

No. Phrase Meaning Suitable Text Types Sample Sentence
1 A double-edged sword Something that has both positive and negative consequences. Argumentative Essays, Letters to the Editor Social media is a double-edged sword; it connects us globally but can also cause severe anxiety.
2 Turn a blind eye to To ignore something that you know is wrong. Editorials, Speeches, Letters The government can no longer turn a blind eye to the worsening housing crisis in the city.
3 Spark a heated debate To cause people to argue passionately about a topic. Articles, Editorials The proposal to build a new landfill in the district has sparked a heated debate among residents.
4 The tip of the iceberg Only a small, visible part of a much larger problem. Reports, Essays, Letters The recent rise in youth crime is merely the tip of the iceberg of deeper societal issues.
5 A blessing in disguise A good thing that seemed bad at first. Blogs, Personal Reflections Failing my first exam was a blessing in disguise because it forced me to change my study habits.
6 Nip (something) in the bud To stop a bad situation before it can develop further. Proposals, Letters to the Editor We must nip school bullying in the bud by promoting empathy in classrooms.
7 Bear the brunt of To suffer the worst part of an unpleasant situation. Articles, Reports Small businesses bore the brunt of the economic downturn during the pandemic.
8 A vicious cycle A repeating situation where one problem causes another, making it worse. Essays, Articles Many poor families are trapped in a vicious cycle of poverty and lack of education.
9 A step in the right direction An action that helps you get closer to a goal. Proposals, Speeches Banning single-use plastics is definitely a step in the right direction for our environment.
10 Open Pandora’s box To do something that causes a lot of new, unexpected problems. Argumentative Essays Legalizing soft drugs might open Pandora’s box, leading to a rise in addiction.
